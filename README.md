# Brennen Lawson - ePortfolio

## Professional Self-Assessment
  Finishing the Computer Science Program has helped me become more confident in my ability to understand technical problems, research solutions, and work through challenges. Developing my ePortfolio gave me an opportunity to revisit my earlier work and recognize how much my approach has changed. Now I pay more attention to how software is organized, how information moves through an application, and how a change could affect other features. My strongest skills are problem-solving, troubleshooting, and continuing to learn and adapt when I come across something I’m not familiar with. 
  
My career interests lean toward IT, but I’m still open to different roles within the computer science field, especially software development. I like understanding how systems work and figuring out why something is not working as it should, and then fixing that issue. I get a great sense of satisfaction with this kind of work. My coursework has given me a foundation for supporting applications, working with databases, investigating technical issues, and much more. Also, it has shaped the values I want to bring to my work: protecting information, communicating clearly, and verifying results before relying on them. My goal is to be somebody who can explain a problem and then help resolve that problem, while recognizing when more assistance and/or research is needed. 

An important part of my progress has been learning to consider the people who will use a system. In my System Analysis and Design course, the DriverPass scenario required me to interpret a client’s requests and then translate them into business requirements and a system design. I had to think about how customers, staff, administrators, and the owner would interact with the proposed platform. This helped me understand that a technically functional system can still fall short if it doesn’t support the way people actually work. Breaking down the requirements and documenting the design gave me practice communicating between a business perspective and a technical one. 

Even though my courses didn’t include a formal team development project, most of the courses had discussion boards where me and my classmates were able to share code, diagrams, planned features and work in progress. These boards gave me opportunities to explain my approach and consider how others approached similar problems. They helped me practice participating in technical conversations and making my work understandable to someone else. I know that working on a professional team will involve additional responsibilities, like coordinating tasks and maintaining shared code. My experience exchanging ideas with classmates gives me a starting point for developing those skills further. 

My coursework also taught me to take larger problems and break them into smaller and more manageable pieces. In CS360, I developed an android application for weight-tracking that I called “Shed-It”. Connecting the screens to stored information made me think beyond the appearance of the interface and understand how user actions affected the application’s data. Building the application in stages and testing it with the Android Emulator helped me identify problems as I added functionality. This experience strengthened my understanding of how interface design, persistence, permissions, and testing all work together. 

I did the same thing in my CS340 course for the Grazioso Salvare dashboard. In this course, I separated MongoDB operations into a reusable Python module, which helped me see the value of keeping database responsibilities separate from display logic. The dashboard connected filtered records to a table, chart, and map, which required attention to both the data and how users would interpret it. This work helped me become more deliberate about organizing code so that individual parts can be understood, reused, and maintained. 

Security eventually became another part of my approach when I took the Software Security course. In the scenario we were given, I had to research dependency vulnerabilities and consider whether reported issues applied to the versions that were being used. Evaluating possible false positives required more judgment than just accepting every warning from a tool. I also practiced configuring HTTPS and checking application behavior after changes. These experiences taught me that security requires investigation and ongoing attention. They encouraged me to consider the consequences of exposing information or trusting input that hasn’t been validated. 

The capstone brought all of these skills together and gave me more experience evaluating design choices with evidence. In particular, working with data structures and algorithms helped me understand that a solution’s usefulness depends on the problem, the amount of data, and the resources available. I compared approaches rather than assuming that a more complex implementation would always perform better. That same habit carried into database indexing, where I examined which queries benefited and which did not. This is an approach I want to continue using professionally: explain the trade-offs, test the assumptions, and make decisions based on the results. 

My ePortfolio centers on Travlr Getaways, which is the application I developed in my Full-Stack Development course. I used this application for all three of the enhancement categories because it allowed me to demonstrate different skills within one connected system. The enhancements build on the original customer website, administrative interface, backend API and MongoDB database. Together, they show how I can extend an existing application while accounting for the behavior that users already depend on. 

The software engineering and design enhancement introduced a feature for comparing two or three travel packages. It required coordinating user selections, validating requests, retrieving current trip information, and presenting the results clearly. The algorithms and data structures enhancement added budget-based trip selection. I implemented a full-sort baseline and a bounded max-heap approach, then checked that both returned the same results. Comparing their performance helped me evaluate the relationship between implementation complexity and potential efficiency gains. 

The database enhancement improved the foundation supporting those features. I introduced validated numeric prices stored in whole cents, developed backup and migration tools, and added a resort pricing report calculated through MongoDB aggregation. I also implemented a separate report permission and evaluated a compound index using 20,000 synthetic records. The index helped the tested resort-filtered queries, while the other query patterns didn’t use it. This is a concrete example of why performance claims need to be tied to a particular workload. 

Verification was a substantial part of the completed work. The final backend test suite passed 136 tests, nine additional checks verified report behavior through MongoDB, and the Angular production build completed successfully. I also manually checked the interfaces and report access restrictions. These results provide evidence for the submitted implementation, while reminding me that testing needs to continue as an application changes. Maintaining consistency across the comparison feature, budget search, trip editing, and report was one of the most valuable challenges of using the same artifact throughout the capstone.

My portfolio presents my growth from completing individual assignments to considering the wider effects of a technical decision. It shows how I connect user needs with application design, choose and evaluate data-processing approaches, and protect the information an application uses. I am still developing my professional experience, and I need to develop it more especially in formal team environments, but I bring a willingness to learn and a methodical approach to solving problems. I want to carry those habits into a role where I can support reliable systems and help people use technology effectively.
